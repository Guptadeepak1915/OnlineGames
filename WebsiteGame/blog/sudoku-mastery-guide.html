Sudoku Solving Techniques: From Singles to Advanced X-Wing

By Deepak Kumar · Published October 2026 · Updated November 2026 · 9 min read

Sudoku is a classic logic-based combinatorial number-placement puzzle that has engaged millions since its modern popularization by Nikoli in Japan. The rules are elegant: fill a 9×9 grid so that every row, column, and 3×3 nonet contains the digits 1 through 9 exactly once.

A properly constructed Sudoku puzzle never requires guessing. Every cell is deterministically solvable via formal deductive logic. Below is a structured curriculum of techniques that will elevate your solving speed from beginner to expert.

1. The Fundamental Level: Naked Singles & Hidden Singles

Naked Singles

A Naked Single occurs when a cell has 8 different numbers visible in its intersecting row, column, and 3×3 box. By pure elimination, only one single candidate number remains legally allowed for that cell. In our Sudoku game, turning on pencil notes highlights these instantly.

Hidden Singles

A Hidden Single occurs when a cell might technically have multiple candidate pencil marks, but a specific digit (say, 7) can only legally fit into that one specific cell within a whole 3×3 box. Even if the cell could hold a 2 or 5, the fact that no other cell in the box can hold a 7 forces that cell to become 7.

2. Intermediate Logic: Naked Pairs & Pointing Pairs

Naked Pairs

When two cells within the same row, column, or 3×3 box contain identical pairs of candidates (e.g. [3, 7] and [3, 7]) and no other candidates, those two digits are locked into those two cells. Therefore, you can safely eliminate 3 and 7 from all other cells in that unit!

Pointing Pairs (Intersection Reduction)

If candidate notes for a digit within a 3×3 box align strictly along a single row or column, that digit must reside within that line inside the box. As a result, you can eliminate that candidate digit from all remaining cells along that row or column outside the box.

3. Advanced Technique: The X-Wing Pattern

The X-Wing is one of the most famous advanced Sudoku patterns. It occurs when a specific candidate digit appears in only two cells in each of two different rows, and those cells share the exact same two columns (forming an imaginary rectangle or "X"):

Row 2: . . [4] . . . . [4] . ← Candidate 4 only in Col 3 & Col 8
Row 7: . . [4] . . . . [4] . ← Candidate 4 only in Col 3 & Col 8

Because Candidate 4 must occupy either the top-left/bottom-right or top-right/bottom-left diagonal corners, Candidate 4 cannot exist in any other cell of Column 3 or Column 8! You can instantly eliminate 4 from all other cells in those two columns, unlocking the rest of the board.

4. Practical Tips for Speed & Mistake Prevention

  • Cross-Hatching: Scan the board digit by digit (from 1 up to 9) across parallel 3×3 boxes to spot immediate hidden singles.
  • Active Pencil Notes: Always mark candidates when a cell has only 2 or 3 possibilities. Avoid marking cells with 5+ candidates as it clutters visual clarity.
  • Use Undo Wisely: In our online game, your lives are limited to 3. If a number conflicts with an existing box, use Undo immediately rather than compounding errors.
