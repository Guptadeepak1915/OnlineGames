Eulerian Paths: The Secret Math of One-Line Drawing Puzzles

By Deepak Kumar · Published November 2026 · 8 min read

In One Line Puzzle (1Line Wood Craft), you are presented with a series of intricate geometric figures on a polished wooden board. Your task appears simple: connect every dot and trace every single segment in one continuous, unbroken stroke without lifting your finger or drawing over any line twice.

While most players attempt trial-and-error, there is a 290-year-old branch of mathematics that solves this entire genre instantly: Graph Theory and Eulerian Trails. Here is how a famous 18th-century problem in East Prussia unlocks every stage in One Line Puzzle.

1. The 1736 Seven Bridges of Königsberg

In 1736, the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler was presented with a famous riddle from the city of Königsberg: Can a pedestrian walk across all seven bridges spanning the Pregel River without crossing any bridge more than once?

Euler proved it was physically impossible. In doing so, he founded topology and graph theory. More importantly, he established the exact mathematical law that governs every modern one-line drawing puzzle.

2. Euler's Universal Theorem for One-Line Puzzles

A connected graph (a network of dots connected by lines) can be drawn in a single continuous stroke without retracing any line if and only if:

Euler's Theorem: The graph must have either zero OR exactly two odd-degree vertices (dots).

What is a Vertex Degree?

The degree of a dot (vertex) is simply the number of lines connected to it:

  • If a dot has 2, 4, or 6 lines connected to it, it is an Even Vertex.
  • If a dot has 1, 3, or 5 lines connected to it, it is an Odd Vertex.

3. How to Know Exactly Where to Start and End

This theorem gives you the ultimate superpower when playing One Line Puzzle:

  • Case A: Exactly Two Odd Vertices. If you count the lines meeting at each dot and find exactly two dots with an odd number of connections, your path MUST start at one of those odd dots and will ALWAYS end at the other odd dot! If you begin your drawing at an even dot, the puzzle is mathematically impossible to complete.
  • Case B: Zero Odd Vertices (All Even). If every single dot has an even number of lines (2, 4, etc.), you can start at any dot on the board, and your path will naturally end back at your starting dot (an Eulerian Circuit)!
  • Case C: More than Two Odd Vertices. The puzzle cannot be solved in a single stroke.

4. Practical In-Game Solving Strategy

When you start a challenging stage in One Line Puzzle, spend 5 seconds scanning the vertices before touching the screen:

  1. Count the connections at each dot.
  2. Identify the two odd dots (usually having 3 or 5 lines).
  3. Place your finger down on one of the odd dots.
  4. Trace toward the outer loops first before cutting across internal bridges.
  5. Watch the golden brass inlay illuminate as you complete the stage effortlessly!
